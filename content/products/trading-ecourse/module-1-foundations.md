## Why Start Here

Every year, thousands of beginners open a brokerage account, deposit money, and start buying things they don't understand — because a friend mentioned a stock, or a video promised quick profits. Most of them lose money. Not because they're unlucky, but because they skipped the foundation.

This module is that foundation. By the end, you'll understand what a stock actually is, what an ETF does, what a cryptocurrency represents, how a trade gets executed, where your money sits, and what realistically happens to returns over time. No picks. No hype. Just the machinery of markets, explained plainly.

## Part 1: What You're Actually Buying

### Stocks

A stock (or share) is a tiny piece of ownership in a company. When a company "goes public," it divides its ownership into millions of shares and sells them to investors through a stock exchange. If you own shares, you own a slice of that business — its profits, its assets, and (sometimes) a vote in how it's run.

Why does a stock's price change? Because buyers and sellers constantly disagree about what the company is worth. If more people want to buy than sell at the current price, the price rises until sellers appear. If more want to sell, it falls until buyers show up. That's it — every price movement in every market boils down to the balance of buying and selling pressure at a given moment.

Stocks come in flavors:
- **Common shares** — the standard type. You get voting rights and a claim on profits (usually paid as dividends, if the company pays them).
- **Preferred shares** — pay a fixed dividend and get paid before common shareholders if the company fails, but usually carry no vote and limited price growth.
- **Blue chips** — shares of large, established companies. Less dramatic movement, generally more stable.
- **Small caps** — shares of smaller companies. More room to grow, but more volatile and more likely to fail.

Key concept: **a stock has no guaranteed value.** A company can prosper or collapse. Shares can pay dividends or pay nothing. The price is a consensus opinion that changes every second the market is open.

### ETFs (Exchange-Traded Funds)

An ETF is a basket of securities — stocks, bonds, commodities, sometimes crypto — bundled into a single product that trades on an exchange like a stock. Buy one share of a broad-market ETF and you instantly own tiny pieces of hundreds of companies.

Why do ETFs exist? Diversification. If you buy one company's stock and it collapses, you can lose a lot. If you own an ETF holding hundreds of companies, one failure barely registers. ETFs also tend to have low fees because many simply track an index (a list of companies, like the S&P 500 or the TSX Composite) rather than paying a manager to pick winners.

Two numbers matter with ETFs:
- **MER (Management Expense Ratio)** — the annual fee, expressed as a percentage. A 0.20% MER means you pay $2 per year for every $1,000 invested. Fees compound against you, so lower is generally better for index ETFs.
- **Tracking** — how closely the ETF follows its index. A good index ETF tracks tightly; the difference is usually just the fee.

ETFs can also be specialized: sector ETFs (only technology, only energy), bond ETFs, commodity ETFs (gold, oil), and leveraged ETFs — which we'll warn you about in Module 2. For now, remember: an ETF is a basket, and baskets spread risk.

### Cryptocurrencies

A cryptocurrency is a digital asset secured by cryptography and recorded on a distributed ledger called a **blockchain** — a shared record kept by thousands of computers instead of one central bank. The first and largest, Bitcoin, was designed as digital money that no government controls. Thousands of others followed, with wildly different purposes: some are platforms for applications, some are pegged to the dollar (called **stablecoins**), and some are, frankly, speculative tokens with no clear use.

Three things make crypto different from stocks:
1. **No underlying company.** A stock is a claim on a business with revenue, assets, and employees. Most cryptocurrencies have no cash flow and no assets — their value comes from adoption, utility, and belief. That makes valuation much harder and prices much more volatile.
2. **24/7 markets.** Stock exchanges close. Crypto trades around the clock, every day of the year — which means prices can move violently while you sleep.
3. **Self-custody is possible (and risky).** You can hold crypto in your own digital wallet, which means no bank can freeze it — but if you lose your private keys, it's gone forever. There is no password reset.

Volatility is the headline feature of crypto: double-digit percentage moves in a single day are routine. We'll cover how to think about that in Module 5.

### How They Compare

| Feature | Stocks | ETFs | Crypto |
|---|---|---|---|
| What you own | Piece of a company | Basket of assets | Digital token on a blockchain |
| Trading hours | Exchange hours, weekdays | Exchange hours, weekdays | 24/7/365 |
| Typical volatility | Moderate | Low–moderate | Very high |
| Dividends possible | Yes (some) | Yes (some) | No (some pay "staking" rewards) |
| Regulation | Heavily regulated | Heavily regulated | Varies by country |

## Part 2: How Markets Actually Work

### Exchanges and the Bid-Ask Spread

Stocks and ETFs trade on **exchanges** — organized marketplaces (like the TSX in Toronto, the NYSE and Nasdaq in the US) where buyers and sellers meet electronically. You never trade "with the exchange"; you trade with other market participants through it.

Every listed security has two prices at any moment:
- The **bid** — the highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay.
- The **ask** — the lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept.

The gap between them is the **spread**. If the bid is $50.00 and the ask is $50.05, the spread is 5 cents. You buy at the ask and sell at the bid, so the spread is a small built-in cost of every trade. Liquid securities (heavily traded ones) have tiny spreads; thinly traded ones have wide spreads, which quietly eat into your returns.

### Market Makers and Liquidity

**Liquidity** means how easily you can buy or sell without moving the price. High liquidity = lots of buyers and sellers = tight spreads and fast fills. **Market makers** are firms that constantly post bids and asks to keep markets liquid; they profit from the spread.

Why should you care? Because illiquid securities can trap you. If you buy something thinly traded and need to sell quickly, you may have to accept a much lower price just to find a buyer. Beginners often learn this the expensive way with penny stocks and obscure tokens.

### Market Hours and Time Zones

North American stock exchanges are generally open weekdays, roughly 9:30 AM–4:00 PM Eastern Time (Toronto and New York share this). There are also pre-market and after-hours sessions with thinner liquidity and wider spreads. Crypto, as noted, never closes.

**Exercise:** Look up the trading hours of your country's main exchange and convert them to your local time zone. Write them down — you'll need them in Module 2.

## Part 3: Brokerages and Account Types

### What a Brokerage Does

A **brokerage** is the company that gives you access to the markets. You open an account, deposit money, and the brokerage routes your orders to the exchange. In exchange it may charge commissions, spreads, or account fees — many Canadian brokerages now offer commission-free stock and ETF trades, making money instead on currency conversion, margin interest, or premium features.

Choosing a brokerage is a practical decision, not a strategy. Compare: fees, the markets you can access (Canadian only? US? international?), the account types offered, the quality of the trading platform, and whether customer support actually answers. A cheap brokerage with a confusing platform can cost you more in mistakes than a slightly pricier one with good tools.

### Canadian Account Types: TFSA, RRSP, Taxable

Where you hold your investments matters as much as what you buy, because Canada taxes the three main account types very differently:

**TFSA (Tax-Free Savings Account)**
- Contributions are made with after-tax dollars (no tax deduction).
- Growth and withdrawals are **tax-free**. This is the big one: if your investments grow inside a TFSA, you owe no tax on the gains, ever.
- You get annual contribution room set by the government, and unused room carries forward. Withdrawals free up room again the following calendar year.
- Best suited for: long-term growth you don't want taxed. One caution: the CRA expects TFSAs to be used for investing, not for running a day-trading business — frequent day trading inside a TFSA can be reclassified as business income and taxed. We'll revisit this in Module 2.

**RRSP (Registered Retirement Savings Plan)**
- Contributions are **tax-deductible** — they reduce your taxable income for the year.
- Growth is tax-sheltered while inside the plan.
- Withdrawals are taxed as income in the year you take them out (that's the trade: deduction now, tax later, ideally in retirement when your income is lower).
- Best suited for: long-term retirement savings, especially in higher-earning years.

**Taxable (Non-Registered) Account**
- No contribution limits, no special tax treatment.
- You pay tax on interest, dividends, and capital gains as they occur. In Canada, only 50% of capital gains are currently included in taxable income (inclusion rate — verify current rules, as governments do change them).
- Best suited for: overflow investing once registered room is used, or money you may need before retirement.

**The beginner takeaway:** account type is a tax decision, not a trading decision. Understand the three buckets before you fund anything, and when in doubt, start with the question "what is this money for?" — the answer usually picks the account.

## Part 4: Order Types — How You Actually Buy and Sell

When you trade, you don't just say "buy." You specify *how*. The three foundational order types:

### Market Order
"Buy/sell right now at the best available price." Fast and simple — but you don't control the exact price. In a fast-moving or illiquid market, the price you get (your **fill**) can differ from the price you saw (this difference is called **slippage**). Market orders prioritize *certainty of execution* over *certainty of price*.

### Limit Order
"Buy/sell only at this price or better." You set a **limit price**: a buy limit fills at your price or lower; a sell limit fills at your price or higher. You control the price — but the trade might never fill if the market never reaches it. Limit orders prioritize *certainty of price* over *certainty of execution*.

### Stop Order (Stop-Loss)
"Once the price hits X, sell/buy at market." A stop-loss is your safety net: you own something at $50, you place a stop at $45, and if the price falls to $45, your position is automatically sold. It converts to a market order when triggered, so in a violent drop you can still get slippage — but it enforces discipline when emotions won't.

A close cousin is the **stop-limit order**: "once the price hits X, place a limit order at Y." More price control, but in a crash it might not fill at all — leaving you holding a falling asset. Know the trade-off.

**The golden rule of order types:** beginners should default to limit orders for entries (never pay more than you decided) and always know where their stop-loss is *before* entering a trade. "I'll figure out my exit later" is how small losses become account-ending ones.

**Exercise:** On paper, "buy" 100 shares of a fictional company at a fictional price using each order type. Write down exactly what happens in three scenarios: price rises 5%, price falls 5%, price gaps down 20% overnight. Notice how the stop-loss behaves differently in the gap scenario.

## Part 5: What Moves Prices

Prices move on **information and expectations**. The main drivers:

1. **Earnings and fundamentals** — companies report profits, revenue, and guidance. Strong results attract buyers; weak results attract sellers. Over the long run, prices loosely follow business performance.
2. **Economic data** — interest rate decisions, inflation reports, employment numbers. Rising rates, for example, make borrowing costlier and future profits worth less today — which is why rate news moves entire markets.
3. **News and events** — product launches, lawsuits, mergers, elections, wars, pandemics. Markets price in expectations *before* events and then adjust to reality — "buy the rumor, sell the news" exists for a reason.
4. **Supply and demand flows** — large funds buying or selling, index rebalances, options expiry. Sometimes prices move for mechanical reasons that have nothing to do with the asset's value.
5. **Sentiment and narratives** — fear and greed, social media hype, compelling stories. Sentiment can detach prices from fundamentals for a long time — "the market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent" is a cliché because it's true.
6. **Liquidity** — in thin markets, small orders move prices a lot. This is why small-cap stocks and small cryptocurrencies swing so violently.

Notice what's missing from this list: **anyone's prediction about tomorrow.** Prices reflect everything known *right now*. Your edge as a learner isn't predicting the future — it's understanding the machinery well enough to manage risk when the future surprises you.

## Part 6: Realistic Expectations — The Math Nobody Shows You

Let's talk honestly about returns and risk, because this is where beginners get hurt.

### What "Good" Returns Actually Look Like

Broad stock markets have historically returned roughly **7–10% per year on average** over long periods, before inflation — and "on average" hides enormous variation. Some years are up 20%; some are down 20%. Anyone promising consistent monthly returns far above that — "make 10% a month!" — is either mistaken or selling something. Compounding is powerful, but it works over years, not weeks.

Quick math to keep you grounded: at 8% per year, money doubles roughly every 9 years (the "rule of 72": 72 ÷ 8 ≈ 9). At a fantasy 50% per month, money would multiply by ~130x in a year — if that were achievable, the person telling you about it would be running the world's largest hedge fund, not selling a course.

### Risk Is the Price of Return

Higher potential returns always come with higher potential losses. This isn't a suggestion; it's structural:
- Government bonds: low return, low risk.
- Broad ETFs: moderate return, moderate risk.
- Individual stocks: higher potential, higher risk.
- Crypto and leveraged products: extreme both ways.

**Volatility** (how much prices swing) and **drawdown** (how far your account falls from its peak) are the costs. A strategy that averages great returns but suffers 50% drawdowns will shake out almost everyone who tries it — including, probably, you. The best strategy is the one you can actually stick with.

### The One Equation That Matters Most

Before you ever trade real money, internalize this:

> **Position size = (Account size × Risk %) ÷ (Entry price − Stop-loss price)**

If you have a $1,000 account, risk 1% per trade ($10), enter at $50 with a stop at $45 ($5 of risk per share), you buy **2 shares**. If the stop hits, you lose $10 — 1% of your account — and you live to trade another day. Ten losing trades in a row costs you ~10%, not your account. *This* is risk management, and Module 6 builds your entire trading plan on it.

### Paper Trade First

**Paper trading** means practicing with fake money on a simulator. It costs nothing and teaches you the mechanics — placing orders, watching positions, feeling the emotional pull of wins and losses — without the tuition fee of real losses. Every professional learned on a simulator, a mentor's desk, or small size. Skip this step and the market will happily charge you for the education instead.
