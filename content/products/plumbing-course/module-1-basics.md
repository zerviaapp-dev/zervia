## Lesson 1: How Residential Plumbing Works — Two Systems, One House

Here's the single most useful thing you'll learn in this course: your home has **two completely separate plumbing systems**, and they work on opposite principles.

**System 1: The supply system.** This brings clean water *into* your home. It's pressurized — typically 40 to 60 psi (pounds per square inch) — which is why water comes flying out of your tap when you open it. The pressure comes from your municipal water supply or your well pump, and it pushes water through pipes to every fixture: sinks, toilets, showers, the dishwasher, the washing machine, the hose bib outside.

Think of it like this: the supply system is a network of highways, and water pressure is the engine pushing every car along. Open a faucet and you've opened an exit ramp — water rushes out because pressure is pushing it from behind.

**System 2: The DWV system — Drain, Waste, Vent.** This takes wastewater *out* of your home. And here's the key difference: it's **not pressurized at all**. It works entirely on gravity. Drain pipes slope gently downward (about a quarter inch of drop per foot of run), and water simply flows downhill to the sewer or septic system.

The "V" — vent — is the part most people have never heard of, and it's genuinely clever. Those pipes sticking out of your roof? They're vents. Every drain needs air behind it to flow properly, just like a straw needs you to let air in at the top. Without vents, drains would gurgle, flow slowly, and trap water in the pipes. Worse, without the venting system, sewer gases could be pushed back up through your drains into your home. Those roof pipes are quietly protecting you every day.

Each fixture also has a **trap** — that U-shaped (or P-shaped) bend under every sink. The trap holds a small amount of water at all times, and that standing water is a seal: it blocks sewer gases from rising up through the drain. This is why a drain that hasn't been used in months can start to smell — the water in the trap evaporated. Run the tap for 30 seconds and the seal is restored. Free fix, and now you know why it works.

**Why this matters for a beginner:** Supply-side problems (leaks) and drain-side problems (clogs) are completely different animals with completely different solutions. A leak means pressurized water escaping — it needs to be stopped at the source, fast. A clog means gravity flow is blocked — it needs to be cleared mechanically. Mixing up which system you're dealing with is how small problems become big ones. From here on, every lesson in this course will tell you which system you're working on.

## Lesson 2: Find Your Main Water Shut-Off (Do This First)

Stop reading and go find it. Seriously — this lesson is a **do it now** lesson, not a "someday" lesson.

Your **main water shut-off valve** controls all water entering your home. In an emergency — a burst pipe, a supply line that lets go behind your washing machine, a toilet that won't stop overflowing — this valve is the difference between a mop-up and a five-figure insurance claim. Water from a burst supply line can dump *hundreds of litres per hour* into your home. Every minute you spend searching for the shut-off is water pouring into your floors, walls, and basement.

**Where to look (Canadian homes):**

- **Basement or utility room:** The most common location. Look where the water line enters the house — usually the wall facing the street. The valve will be on that pipe, often near the water meter.
- **Crawl space:** In homes without a full basement, check the crawl space near the entry point. (Bring a flashlight. And maybe a friend.)
- **Near the water heater or furnace:** Sometimes clustered with other utilities.
- **Outside:** In warmer climates, or in some condos and townhouses, there may be a shut-off in a ground-level box near the property line. In most of Canada, the main shut-off is indoors because of freezing.

**What it looks like:** There are two common types.

- **Ball valve:** A lever handle. When the lever is *parallel* to the pipe, water is ON. Turn it 90 degrees so it's *perpendicular* to the pipe, and water is OFF. These are the good ones — a quarter turn and you're done.
- **Gate valve:** A round wheel handle, like a garden hose spigot. Turn clockwise to close. These are older and can be stiff or seize up if they haven't been moved in years.

**The "test it on a calm Saturday" rule:** Don't discover your shut-off is seized at 2 AM with water pouring through your ceiling. Go turn it off *right now*, on a calm day, and confirm the water stops (open a tap — it should slow to a trickle and stop). Then turn it back on. If it's stiff, work it gently back and forth — don't force it. If it won't budge or it leaks when you operate it, that's a job for a plumber *before* an emergency, and it's a cheap one compared to the alternative.

**Renters:** You may not have access to the main shut-off (it's sometimes in a locked utility room). Find out from your landlord or building manager where it is and who to call. At minimum, know your *fixture* shut-offs (next lesson).

**Your homework:** Fill in the Shut-Off Valve Locator Worksheet (included with this course). Write down the exact location of your main shut-off, what type of valve it is, and the date you tested it. Stick a copy on the inside of a kitchen cabinet door. Future-you, ankle-deep in water at midnight, will be grateful.

## Lesson 3: Fixture Shut-Offs — Your First Line of Defense

Before you reach for the main shut-off, there's usually a closer option: the **fixture shut-off valve**. These are the small valves on the supply lines serving individual fixtures — the little knobs under your bathroom and kitchen sinks, and the one on the wall or floor behind your toilet.

They look like small oval or round handles (newer ones are quarter-turn levers) on the hot and cold lines feeding each fixture. Their job is simple: let you shut off water to *one fixture* without killing water to the whole house. Replacing a bathroom faucet? Shut off the two valves under that sink and the rest of the house keeps running.

**The catch:** These valves often haven't been touched in years — sometimes decades. Rubber washers inside them dry out and harden. The first time you turn one, it may drip, or the handle may snap off, or it may not fully close. This is normal and fixable, but it's why you **exercise them gently before you need them**:

1. Place a towel and a small bucket or bowl underneath.
2. Turn the valve clockwise to close it — gently. If it resists, don't force it; a quarter-turn ball valve should move easily, an older multi-turn valve may need patience.
3. Open the fixture's tap to confirm the water stops. A brief trickle that stops is fine; a steady flow means the valve isn't sealing.
4. Turn it back on.

If a fixture shut-off leaks from the handle stem when you operate it, try tightening the small packing nut just behind the handle a quarter turn — that often stops the weep. If the valve won't close fully or the handle breaks, leave it alone and call a plumber to replace it. That's a small, cheap service call — and now you know about it *before* the emergency.

**The golden rule of this course, stated for the first time (it will be repeated):** Every repair walkthrough begins with shutting off the water and *verifying* it's off. "I think it's off" is not off. Open the tap. Watch the water stop. Then start work.

## Lesson 4: Water Pressure Basics

Water pressure is invisible, and that's what makes it dangerous to your plumbing. Too little and your shower is a sad drizzle. Too much and it silently destroys fixtures, supply lines, valves, and your water heater from the inside out.

**What's normal:** Residential water pressure should be between **40 and 60 psi**. Below 40, you'll notice weak flow. Above 80 psi, most plumbing codes say you *must* have a pressure-reducing valve (PRV) — and there's a reason: sustained high pressure blows out toilet fill valves, dishwasher solenoids, washing machine hoses, and faucet cartridges. If your home eats through faucet cartridges or you keep replacing toilet fill valves, high pressure is suspect number one.

**How to check:** A water pressure gauge costs about $15 CAD at any hardware store. Screw it onto an outdoor hose bib (or the laundry tap), make sure no water is running anywhere in the house, and read the dial. That's it — a two-minute test that tells you a lot.

**What you can do about it:**
- **Too high (over 75–80 psi):** This is a plumber job — installing or adjusting a pressure-reducing valve where the main line enters the house. Don't ignore it; high pressure is a slow-motion flood.
- **Too low (under 40 psi):** Check whether it's the whole house or one fixture. One fixture? Probably a clogged aerator (unscrew it, rinse it — five minutes). Whole house? Could be a partially closed main valve, a failing PRV, or a municipal supply issue — start with the easy checks, then call a pro.

**One more pressure concept:** **Water hammer** — that loud bang or thud when a washing machine or dishwasher valve snaps shut. It's pressure shock waves in the pipes, and over time it loosens joints and damages valves. If you hear it regularly, mention it to a plumber; the fix (water hammer arrestors) is straightforward for a professional.

## Lesson 5: Pipe Materials You'll Encounter

Open up a wall or look under a sink in any Canadian home and you'll meet one of these. You need to be able to *identify* them — not work on all of them.

**Copper.** The classic. Rigid, reddish-brown metal pipes joined with soldered fittings (you'll see the silvery solder rings at joints). Extremely durable — 50+ year lifespan. **Beginner rule: do not cut, solder, or modify copper.** Soldering requires a torch, and a torch near wood framing is how houses catch fire. Leave copper work to plumbers.

**PEX (cross-linked polyethylene).** Flexible plastic tubing, usually red (hot), blue (cold), or white. It's become the standard for new work and repipes because it's flexible, freeze-resistant, and fast to install. You'll see it snaking through basements and utility rooms. **Beginner rule: do not cut or crimp PEX.** The fittings require special crimp or expansion tools and the connections must be exactly right — a bad crimp is a slow leak inside your wall.

**PVC and ABS.** Rigid plastic pipes — white (PVC) or black (ABS) — used for *drain, waste, and vent* lines. You'll see them under sinks (the drain assembly) and in basements. These are the most DIY-accessible pipes because drain connections are low-pressure and often use slip joints and threaded fittings you can hand-tighten. Still: anything inside a wall or below a slab is pro territory.

**Galvanized steel.** Older homes (pre-1970s, roughly). Dull grey metal pipes, threaded fittings. They corrode from the inside out, restricting flow — if your old house has weak pressure at every fixture, galvanized pipe is a likely culprit. **Beginner rule: do not touch.** The fittings are often corroded in place; disturbing them can crack a joint three feet away that you can't see.

**The takeaway:** As a beginner, your work zone is *fixtures and their connections* — faucets, supply lines, toilet tanks, traps, and drain fittings you can reach and see. The pipe *system* itself — inside walls, under slabs, the main line — belongs to licensed plumbers. That's not a limitation; it's the whole point of the "know your limits" framework below.

## Lesson 6: Safety Rules — Water, Electricity, and Judgment

Plumbing safety isn't about fear. It's about respect for two facts: water destroys homes quietly, and water plus electricity kills.

**Water damage is the real risk.** A small leak you can't see — inside a wall, under a floor — can cause thousands of dollars in damage before you notice the stain on the ceiling. This is why this course hammers three habits: shut off the water first, verify it's off, and check your work (including a 24-hour watch on any repair). A repair isn't done when the wrench goes down; it's done when you've confirmed it's dry a day later.

**Water + electricity awareness.** Water conducts electricity. Before working near or under a sink with a garbage disposal or dishwasher connection, know where the breaker is. If water is actively leaking onto or near electrical outlets, panels, or appliances — **do not touch the water, do not touch the electrical panel with wet hands or while standing in water.** Shut off the main water if you safely can, get out, and call for help. If there's any chance of electrical hazard, call an electrician or emergency services first.

**Protect yourself, too.** Wear gloves when working with drain water (it's genuinely unsanitary — that's what the DWV system is carrying). Eye protection when you're under a sink looking up at fittings you're loosening. And never, ever use chemical drain cleaners as a first resort — they can cause chemical burns, damage pipes, and create a hazardous situation for anyone (including a future plumber) who opens that drain.

**Gas lines: the hard line.** If your water heater is gas-fired, you will see a gas line near your plumbing. **Never touch gas lines, gas valves (other than the appliance shut-off in a true emergency you already understand), or gas water heater internals.** Gas work is licensed-professional-only, everywhere, no exceptions, no "it's just a small fitting." This course will never ask you to go near gas.

## Lesson 7: The "Know Your Limits" Framework

This is the most important lesson in the course. Memorize it.

Every plumbing task falls into one of three zones:

**🟢 GREEN — Safe for a careful beginner.** Replacing a faucet cartridge or aerator. Replacing a toilet flapper or fill valve. Tightening or replacing a braided supply line. Plunging a drain or toilet. Using a hand auger on a sink drain. Replacing a P-trap under a sink (slip-joint fittings only). These are the jobs this course teaches you.

**🟡 YELLOW — Proceed only if you're confident AND the shut-off works.** Anything where you've verified the water is fully off, you can see the entire work area, and you have the right parts on hand. If any of those three conditions fails, the job drops to red.

**🔴 RED — Call a licensed plumber. Every time.** Main water line work. Sewer line issues or backups (sewage is a health hazard — not a DIY project). Anything involving gas lines. Water heater internals (tank or tankless). Any job requiring a permit under your local code. Anything inside a wall or under a slab. And the universal rule: **if water is flowing where it shouldn't and you can't stop it, you're already past the DIY zone.**

**The framework in one sentence:** *If you can see the whole job, you can stop the water completely, and the worst case is a wet towel — proceed carefully. If any of that isn't true, call a pro.*

There's no shame in the red zone. The most expensive plumbing disasters don't come from people who knew nothing — they come from people who knew *just enough* to start a job they couldn't finish. Knowing when to call a plumber isn't the opposite of DIY skill. It *is* DIY skill — the most valuable part of it.

## Module 1 Practice Exercises

1. **Locate and test your main water shut-off.** Time yourself. If it took more than two minutes to find, that's exactly why this exercise exists.
2. **Complete the Shut-Off Valve Locator Worksheet.** Map every fixture shut-off in your home.
3. **Buy a pressure gauge and test your water pressure.** Write down the reading and the date. Recheck yearly.
4. **Identify your pipe materials.** Look under two sinks and at your water heater connections. Can you name what you see?
5. **Find your water heater's data plate.** Note the age. (Water heaters typically last 8–12 years — knowing the age tells you whether "replace soon" is on your horizon.)
