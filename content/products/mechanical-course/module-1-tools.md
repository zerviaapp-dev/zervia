## 1.1 Meet Your Hand Tools

Every mechanical skill starts with hand tools. Not the hundred-piece mega set from the big box store — the core few that handle 90% of real work. Here's what each one does, and how to choose a good one without overspending.

**The essentials:**

- **Screwdrivers (flathead + Phillips).** They tighten and loosen screws. Quality matters more here than anywhere: cheap screwdrivers have soft tips that strip screws. Buy ones with hardened tips and a comfortable grip. Get a set with multiple sizes — using the wrong size is how screws get stripped.
- **Adjustable wrench (also called a crescent wrench).** Grips nuts and bolts of various sizes. Great for beginners because one tool does many jobs. Real talk from the field: adjustable wrenches round off bolt corners if you force them, so once you know your common sizes, upgrade to fixed combination wrenches for the work you do most.
- **Combination wrenches (fixed sizes).** One end open, one end a ring ("box"). The box end grips better and won't slip. In Canada, you'll need BOTH metric and imperial sets — more on that in section 1.4.
- **Pliers (needle-nose + slip-joint).** For gripping, bending, and holding small parts. Needle-nose reaches into tight spots. Slip-joint adjusts for bigger things.
- **Hammer (claw hammer).** For driving nails, but also for tapping things into alignment. The claw end pulls nails. Strike with the face, controlled — a hammer swung wildly is how fingers get crushed.
- **Tape measure.** You'll use this constantly. Get one with clear markings in both inches and centimetres (standard in Canada). Learn to read to the 1/16 inch and the millimetre — we'll cover reading a tape properly in the civil course, and the skill transfers directly here.
- **Level.** Tells you if something is horizontal or vertical. A 2-foot level covers most household jobs.
- **Hex keys (Allen keys).** L-shaped keys for socket-head screws — the ones that hold together most flat-pack furniture, bicycles, and machinery guards. Get a set; losing them is a rite of passage.

**How to choose quality:** Pick up the tool. It should feel solid and balanced, not hollow or flimsy. Check that moving parts (wrench jaws, plier pivots) move smoothly without wobble. For screwdrivers and wrenches, look for chrome vanadium or similar alloy steel markings. You don't need the most expensive brand — but avoid the cheapest set on the shelf. A mid-range set from a reputable brand will outlast three cheap sets and cost less in total.

**Tool care:** Wipe tools clean after use, especially if they touched grease or moisture. A light oil wipe on metal surfaces prevents rust. Store them dry and organized — a toolbox or even a labeled bucket beats a drawer where everything bangs together. Tools you can't find might as well not exist.

## 1.2 Power Tool Basics and Safety

Power tools multiply your capability — and your risk. The safety rules below aren't suggestions; they exist because professionals who skipped them got hurt.

**The three beginner power tools:**

- **Cordless drill/driver.** Drills holes and drives screws. The most useful power tool you'll own. Learn the difference between the drill mode and the driver/clutch mode — the clutch lets you set a torque limit so you don't strip screws (ties directly into section 1.5).
- **Circular saw.** Cuts lumber and sheet goods in straight lines. Powerful and fast. Never force it — let the blade do the cutting at its own pace.
- **Angle grinder.** Cuts and grinds metal (and masonry with the right disc). Probably the most dangerous common tool: the disc spins at 10,000+ RPM and can shatter if misused. Wear a face shield, not just safety glasses.

**The safety rules that actually matter:**

1. **Eye protection, every time.** Chips, dust, and broken bits fly at face level. Prescription glasses don't count as safety glasses.
2. **Unplug or remove the battery before changing bits/blades.** Every single time. "It'll be fine" is what people say right before it isn't.
3. **Secure your workpiece.** Clamp it. A board that kicks back or spins is the most common injury cause with drills and saws.
4. **Keep hands away from the cutting path.** Know where the blade goes, and where your fingers are. If you have to ask "is my hand too close?" — it is.
5. **Read the manual.** Boring advice that prevents exciting injuries. The manual tells you the tool's limits — max disc size, proper blade type, duty cycle.

**A real example from QA work:** We've reviewed incident reports where a worker used a grinding disc on a tool rated for cutting discs only — the disc shattered and the fragments embedded in the wall. The tool, the disc, and the guard all matter as a system. Use the right accessory for the right tool, always.

## 1.3 Fasteners Decoded — Bolts, Screws, Nuts, Washers, Anchors

Fasteners hold the built world together — literally. Understanding them is one of the highest-value skills in this course.

**Bolts vs. screws (the real distinction):**
- A **bolt** is designed to go through parts and be secured with a nut on the other side. It clamps things together.
- A **screw** threads into the material itself (wood, metal, plastic) and holds by gripping.
- Quick test: if there's a nut on the other end, it's a bolt. If it disappears into the material, it's a screw.

**Common fastener types:**

- **Hex bolts.** Six-sided head, used with a wrench. The workhorse of machinery and structural connections.
- **Machine screws.** Smaller, precise, used in equipment and electronics. Usually need a nut or a threaded hole.
- **Wood screws.** Tapered body with sharp threads that bite into wood. The tapered shape is the giveaway.
- **Self-tapping screws.** Cut their own threads into sheet metal or plastic. Common in HVAC ductwork and appliances.
- **Lag bolts (lag screws).** Big, beefy screws for heavy wood connections — deck posts, ledgers, machinery to wooden skids.
- **Nuts.** The partner to bolts. Hex nuts are standard; lock nuts (with a nylon insert or deformed threads) resist vibrating loose — critical on anything that shakes, like engines and motors.
- **Washers.** Flat rings that sit under the bolt head or nut. They spread the load so the fastener doesn't dig into the material, and they protect the surface. Lock washers add grip against loosening (though engineers debate their effectiveness — a lock nut is usually the better call).
- **Anchors.** For fastening into concrete, brick, or drywall where there's nothing to thread into. Concrete wedge anchors and sleeve anchors for masonry; toggle bolts and plastic expansion anchors for drywall. Using the wrong anchor is why shelves fall off walls.

**Reading a fastener label:** A bolt marked "M10 x 1.5 x 50" is a metric bolt: 10 mm diameter, 1.5 mm thread pitch, 50 mm long. An imperial bolt marked "3/8-16 x 2" is 3/8 inch diameter, 16 threads per inch, 2 inches long. Once you can read these, you can walk into any hardware store and buy exactly what you need.

## 1.4 Threads: Metric vs. Imperial — Why Canada Needs Both

This matters more in Canada than almost anywhere else, and it's a constant source of confusion on job sites.

**The two systems:**
- **Metric threads** are measured in millimetres. M8, M10, M12 — the number is the diameter. Thread pitch (the distance between threads) is also in millimetres, e.g., 1.25 mm.
- **Imperial threads** are measured in inches. 1/4", 3/8", 1/2" — and thread pitch is given as threads per inch (TPI), e.g., 20 TPI.

**Why Canada has both:** Canada officially went metric in the 1970s, so modern Canadian and most international equipment uses metric. But the US — Canada's biggest trading partner and the source of much of our equipment, vehicles, and building hardware — still uses imperial. The result: a typical Canadian workshop, job site, or garage has BOTH. A North American car might have metric engine bolts and imperial body hardware. Older buildings run imperial; newer equipment runs metric.

**The golden rule: never force a mismatched thread.** A metric nut will start onto an imperial bolt and feel almost right — then seize, strip, or cross-thread. If a fastener doesn't thread on smoothly by hand for the first few turns, STOP. Check whether you have the right system. Cross-threading a bolt into an expensive piece of equipment turns a 30-second job into a multi-hundred-dollar repair.

**Practical tip:** Buy a thread pitch gauge (a few dollars) and a cheap digital caliper. Measure the diameter, check the pitch, and you'll never guess again. Keep your metric and imperial fasteners in SEPARATE, labeled containers. Mixing them in one bin is how a Saturday afternoon disappears.

## 1.5 Torque Basics — How Tight Is Tight Enough?

Torque is the twisting force you apply when tightening a fastener, measured in Newton-metres (N·m) or foot-pounds (ft·lb). Getting it right is one of the most underappreciated mechanical skills there is.

**Why it matters — real consequences:**

- **Under-tightening:** The fastener loosens over time, especially with vibration. We've seen equipment where under-torqued bolts backed out and a guard panel fell onto a walkway. On a vehicle, under-torqued lug nuts can let a wheel come off. On a flange, under-tightening causes leaks.
- **Over-tightening:** Stretches the bolt past its limit, strips threads, cracks the parts being clamped, or snaps the bolt outright. Over-tightened drain plugs strip oil pans (a $500+ mistake). Over-tightened bolts on a gasket crush it and CAUSE the leak they were meant to prevent.

**The right way:** Use a **torque wrench**. It's a wrench with a gauge or click mechanism that tells you when you've reached the specified torque. Set the value, tighten until it clicks, stop. That's it.

**Where do you find the torque value?** Equipment manuals, service manuals, and manufacturer specs list them. For common fasteners, torque charts by size and grade are widely available (and printed in our course reference chart). A general rule: the bigger and stronger the bolt, the higher the torque — but always check the spec rather than guessing.

**Torque sequence matters too:** When tightening multiple bolts on one part (a wheel, a flange, a cylinder head), don't go around in a circle. Tighten in a **star/cross pattern**, in stages (snug all first, then half torque, then full torque). This seats the part evenly. Tightening one side fully first warps flanges and cracks castings — another failure mode we've seen in the field more than once.

**"Tight enough" for everyday work:** For non-critical household fasteners, "snug plus a quarter turn" is a reasonable rule of thumb — firm, not heroic. But anything involving safety (wheels, structural connections, pressure equipment), always use a torque wrench and the specified value.

## 1.6 Tool Care and Storage — Protect Your Investment

Good tools last decades. Neglected tools last months. The difference is minutes of care.

- **Clean after use.** Wipe off grease, dust, and moisture. For power tools, blow out vents with compressed air or a brush — clogged vents overheat motors.
- **Prevent rust.** A light coat of oil on bare metal surfaces (wrench jaws, saw blades, drill bits) stops rust before it starts. In a damp garage, this matters enormously.
- **Sharpen and maintain.** Drill bits can be sharpened; saw blades can be replaced or professionally sharpened. A dull tool is a dangerous tool — it requires more force, slips more, and gives worse results.
- **Store properly.** Hang hand tools on a pegboard or keep them in a toolbox with dividers. Store power tools in their cases with batteries removed. Keep everything dry — a dehumidifier or even silica packs in a damp workshop pays for itself.
- **Batteries:** Store cordless tool batteries at partial charge (not full, not empty) in a cool, dry place. Don't leave them on the charger indefinitely.

**The mindset:** In QA and inspection work, we treat tools as instruments — because they are. A tape measure that's been dropped and bent gives wrong measurements. A torque wrench that's never been calibrated gives wrong torque. Respect the tool and it respects the work.
