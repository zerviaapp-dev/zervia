# Lesson 1: Greetings & Introductions
### Module 2 — Your first real Hausa conversations

Welcome to your first lesson! If you learn nothing else in Hausa, learn to greet well — because in Hausa culture, greetings aren't small talk. They're the relationship. A proper greeting tells people you respect them, and it opens every door: the market stall, the taxi, the family compound.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to say hello at any time of day, introduce yourself, ask someone's name, say please and thank you, and say goodbye warmly. That's a real conversation — and you're about to have it.

**A quick pronunciation note before we start:** Hausa vowels are pure, like in Spanish or Italian: **a** = "ah," **i** = "ee," **u** = "oo," **e** = "eh," **o** = "oh." Read the guides below out loud — your mouth learns faster than your eyes.

---

## 15 essential greetings & introductions

### Saying hello

**1. Sannu!** — *SAHN-noo*
*Hello!*
The single most useful word in Hausa. Works anytime, anywhere, with anyone. If you remember one word from this course, make it this one.

**2. Lafiya lau?** — *lah-FEE-yah LOW*
*Are you well? / Is there peace?*
The classic follow-up to *Sannu*. *Lafiya* means peace, health, wellness — asking after it is asking after everything that matters.

**3. Lafiya kalau.** — *lah-FEE-yah kah-LOW*
*I'm very well.*
The warm standard reply. Even on a rough day, this is what you say — optimism is part of the ritual.

**4. Barka da safiya** — *BAHR-kah dah sah-FEE-yah*
*Good morning* (literally "blessing of the morning")
Polite and slightly formal — perfect for elders, colleagues, and anyone you want to impress. *Barka* = blessing; you'll meet this word in greetings for every occasion.

**5. Ina kwana** — *EE-nah KWAH-nah*
*Good morning* (informal; literally "how was the night?")
What friends, neighbors, and market traders actually say to each other in the morning. Warm and everyday.

**6. Ina yini** — *EE-nah yee-NEE*
*Good afternoon* (literally "how is the day?")
The afternoon equivalent — used from midday until late afternoon.

**7. Ina wuni** — *EE-nah WOO-nee*
*Good evening / How was your day?*
For the evening hours. Notice the pattern: Hausa greets the *time itself* — a lovely window into how the language thinks.

### Introducing yourself

**8. Sunanka? / Sunanki?** — *soo-NAHN-kah / soo-NAHN-kee*
*What is your name?*
Use **sunanka** when speaking to a man or boy, **sunanki** to a woman or girl. (Hausa marks "you" for gender: *ka* = you-male, *ki* = you-female. You'll get used to it quickly.)

**9. Sunana ___** — *soo-NAH-nah*
*My name is ___.*
Just add your name: *Sunana Sarah.* Simple, and it works every time.

**10. Daga ina kake? / Daga ina kike?** — *DAH-gah EE-nah KAH-keh / KEE-keh*
*Where are you from?*
Again, **kake** for a man, **kike** for a woman. Answer with *Daga ___ nake* (I'm from ___): *Daga London nake.*

### Please, thanks, excuse me

**11. Na gode** — *nah GOH-deh*
*Thank you* (literally "I am grateful")
The straightforward, always-correct thank you. Use it freely — gratitude is never out of place.

**12. Madalla!** — *mah-DAHL-lah*
*Bravo! Well done!*
Said with real warmth when someone does something good for you — brings food, helps you, gives a great answer. It lands like a heartfelt "thank you, that's wonderful!" and always earns a smile.

**13. Don Allah** — *dohn ahl-LAH*
*Please* (literally "for God's sake")
The universal softener for requests: *Don Allah, bani ruwa* (please, give me water). It carries gentle religious weight in this deeply Muslim culture — which is exactly why it works so well as "please."

**14. Yi hakuri** — *yee hah-KOO-ree*
*Excuse me / I'm sorry / Forgive me*
For bumping into someone, interrupting, or apologizing. (Note the little catch in the throat on *hak'uri* — that's the glottal stop, one of Hausa's special sounds from Module 1.)

### Welcome & goodbye

**15. Sannu da zuwa / Barka da zuwa** — *SAHN-noo dah ZOO-wah / BAHR-kah dah ZOO-wah*
*Welcome* (literally "greetings on arriving" / "blessing on arriving")
What you'll hear when you arrive somewhere — a shop, a home, a town. The proper reply is a big smile and *Na gode!*

**16. Sai an jima** — *sigh ahn JEE-mah*
*See you later / Goodbye*
The everyday farewell. For "until tomorrow morning," say **Sai da safe** (*sigh dah SAH-feh*) — a lovely touch when leaving someone in the evening.

## Mini-dialogue 1: Meeting someone new

*Two strangers meet in the morning.*

**A:** Barka da safiya!
*Good morning!*

**B:** Barka da safiya. Sannu!
*Good morning. Hello!*

**A:** Lafiya lau?
*Are you well?*

**B:** Lafiya kalau. Sunanka?
*Very well. What's your name?*

**A:** Sunana Aisha. Daga ina kike?
*My name is Aisha. Where are you from?*

**B:** Daga Kano nake. Sannu da zuwa!
*I'm from Kano. Welcome!*

Notice how the greeting comes *before* the names — in Hausa, you establish goodwill first, identity second.

---

## Mini-dialogue 2: The famous greeting rounds

*Neighbors meet in the afternoon. Watch how one "hello" becomes a whole exchange — this is normal, and it's beautiful.*

**A:** Sannu!
*Hello!*

**B:** Lafiya lau!
*Peace! (Are you well?)*

**A:** Lafiya kalau! Iyalan?
*Very well! And the family?*

**B:** Lafiya! Yaran?
*Well! And the children?*

**A:** Suna lafiya. Aiki?
*They're well. And work?*

**B:** Da sauki. Sannu!
*All fine. Greetings!*

**A:** Sannu!
*Greetings!*

This back-and-forth — *Sannu… Lafiya… Iyalan… Yaran… Aiki…* — can run several rounds. Don't rush it, don't cut it short. Each round says: *you matter enough for my time.* When someone greets you this way, answer every round. That's the whole secret.

---

## Mini-dialogue 3: Saying goodbye

*Leaving a shop in the evening.*

**You:** Na gode! Madalla!
*Thank you! Wonderful!*

**Shopkeeper:** Madalla! Sai an jima!
*Bravo to you too! See you later!*

**You:** Sai da safe!
*Until tomorrow morning!*

A: ______ lau?

*(Answers: Barka · Sannu · Lafiya · kalau · Suna · Suna)*

### Part C — Say it out loud (3 minutes)
Stand up and say Dialogue 1 aloud, playing both parts. Focus on:
- Pure vowels: *SAHN-noo*, not "san-oo"
- The throat-catch in *hak'uri* (from *Yi hakuri*)
- The musical rise on questions: *Lafiya lau?* goes UP at the end

Say it three times. Speed doesn't matter — warmth does.

### Part D — Your first mission 🎯
Today, greet *someone* in Hausa — a Hausa-speaking friend, a colleague, a shopkeeper, or even yourself in the mirror. Start with *Sannu!*, follow with *Lafiya lau?*, and see what happens. If they answer *Lafiya kalau*, you've just had your first real Hausa exchange. Well done — *Madalla!*

## Cultural note: the greeting ritual 🤝

**Take your time.** In Hausa culture, rushing a greeting is like skipping a handshake mid-pump — it feels wrong. When someone greets you, stop, face them, and answer fully. The rounds aren't inefficiency; they're respect made audible.

**Elders first, elders longest.** Always greet older people first in a group, and give them extra rounds. The respectful salutation *Ranka ya daɗe* (to a man) / *Ranki ya daɗe* (to a woman) — "may your life be long" — is a beautiful touch for someone clearly senior.

**Right hand, always.** Give, receive, wave, and gesture with your right hand. The left hand is considered unclean — this matters enormously, so build the habit now.

**Greeting across genders.** Northern Nigeria is predominantly Muslim and often conservative. Men and women who aren't related typically keep greetings **verbal** — a warm *Sannu!* with a nod or hand-on-chest, not a handshake. Don't extend your hand to someone of the opposite gender unless they extend theirs first; simply follow their lead. Nobody will think less of you for a respectful verbal greeting — quite the opposite.

**Fridays are special.** Friday (*Juma'a*) is the holy day. *Barka da Juma'a* — "blessed Friday" — on a Friday morning will make you instant friends.

**Ask about people, not just the person.** *Iyalan?* (the family?), *Yaran?* (the children?), *Lafiya?* — asking after someone's people signals that you see them as embedded in a community, which is exactly how Hausa culture sees everyone. It's the fastest way from "foreigner" to "friend."
