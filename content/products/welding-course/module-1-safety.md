# Module 1: Welding Safety Essentials

*This module is required before continuing with the rest of the course.*

---

## 1.1 Why Welding Safety Is Non-Negotiable

Welding is honest work with honest hazards. Every year, welders and nearby workers are injured — and some killed — by hazards that are entirely preventable: eye damage from unprotected arc exposure, fires from hot work, respiratory illness from years of breathing metal fumes, and electrical shock from damaged equipment.

Here is the framing that matters: **every welding injury is the result of a hazard that was known about in advance.** None of these are surprises. The industry has decades of data on exactly what goes wrong, and every prevention method in this module is the result of someone learning the hard way so you don't have to.

Safety in welding isn't about being timid. It's about being professional. The best welders you'll ever meet are the most safety-conscious people on the site — because they understand that their body is the most important tool they own, and they intend to keep it working for a long career.

## 1.2 PPE Head to Toe

Your personal protective equipment is your last line of defense. It doesn't replace safe practices — it protects you when something goes wrong anyway. Here is the full kit, head to toe:

**Head and face — the welding helmet.** This is the single most important item. An auto-darkening helmet is the modern standard, and the shade number matters: for most arc welding (SMAW, GMAW, FCAW), you need **shade 10 to 13** depending on the amperage. Higher amperage means a darker shade. GTAW at low amperage can use shade 8. Never look at an arc with an undarkened helmet or unprotected eyes — not even for a "quick peek." Arc flash damage is cumulative and instantaneous exposure can cause injury.

**Eyes under the helmet.** Wear safety glasses with side shields *under* your welding helmet. Chips fly when you're chipping slag or grinding. Your helmet's lens can crack or be lifted at the wrong moment. Safety glasses are cheap; retinas are not.

**Ears.** Fire-resistant ear plugs or muffs. Grinding is loud enough to cause hearing damage, and ear protection doubles as protection against sparks and hot spatter landing in the ear canal — which is as painful as it sounds.

**Hands — welding gloves.** Heavy leather gloves are the standard. SMAW and FCAW throw more spatter and heat, so thicker, longer-cuffed gloves are typical. GTAW needs thinner, more dexterous gloves since you're feeding filler wire by hand. Whatever the process, never weld with gloves that are soaked with oil or have holes burned through them — damaged gloves are just expensive hand warmers.

**Body — jacket and clothing.** A flame-resistant (FR) welding jacket or leather apron over FR-rated clothing. The critical rule: **no synthetic fabrics.** Polyester, nylon, and blends can melt into the skin when struck by spatter. Cotton is better; purpose-made FR garments are best. Long sleeves, always. A collar or bandana protects the neck from UV burns — yes, you can get sunburned by an arc, and yes, it hurts exactly like a bad sunburn.

**Legs and feet — boots.** Leather, high-top (8-inch) boots with metatarsal guards if available. Steel-toe or composite-toe. Laces must be covered or tucked — laces are a spark magnet, and a smoldering lace you can't see is a fire waiting to happen. Trousers go *over* the boot tops so spatter rolls off instead of pooling inside the boot.

**Respiratory protection.** This deserves its own section — see 1.4. But as PPE: a respirator rated for metal fumes (typically a P100 half-face respirator, or supplied air in confined spaces) whenever ventilation is insufficient. Fit-testing matters; a respirator that doesn't seal doesn't protect.

**What to buy, what to avoid.** Buy PPE from reputable manufacturers that meet recognized standards (look for ANSI, CSA, or equivalent markings). Avoid: helmets with no shade rating, "welding gloves" that are just gardening gloves, any eye protection not rated for UV/IR. If it's cheap enough to seem like a bargain, it's not protection — it's decoration.

## 1.3 Arc Flash & Eye Damage

**Arc eye** (photokeratitis) is a burn of the cornea caused by intense ultraviolet light from the welding arc. It feels like sand in the eyes, often hours after exposure — you might not know you're hurt until that night. Symptoms include pain, redness, tearing, and temporary vision loss. It's extremely painful and, with repeated exposure, can cause lasting damage.

**It is not just your eyes at risk.** Anyone within line of sight of an arc — the welder's helper, the laborer walking by, the inspector watching the weld — needs protection. This is why:

- **Never strike an arc without your helmet down.** No exceptions. Not "just a tack." Not "just a second."
- **Protect bystanders.** Welding screens or curtains (opaque, UV-blocking) around the work area are standard on any professional site. If someone could see your arc, they could be hurt by it.
- **Warn before striking.** A simple "eyes!" or "striking an arc!" callout is professional courtesy that prevents injuries.
- **Reflected arc light counts.** Arc light bouncing off walls, floors, or polished metal still carries UV. Enclosed spaces multiply this effect.

Also know: prolonged UV exposure from welding can burn exposed skin the same way sunlight does, which is why full skin coverage is the rule. Long-term, chronic UV exposure is associated with skin cancer. Cover everything.

## 1.4 Fume Hazards & Ventilation

Welding fumes are not "just smoke." They are fine metal particles and gases created when the arc vaporizes the electrode and base metal. What you breathe depends on what you're welding and with what — but it is never nothing.

**Two to know by name:**

- **Manganese.** Present in many steels and welding consumables. Chronic overexposure is linked to neurological effects resembling Parkinson's disease. This is not theoretical — it is documented in career welders who worked without adequate ventilation.
- **Hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)).** Generated when welding stainless steel and some other alloys. A known carcinogen. Welding stainless without proper fume control is one of the highest-exposure scenarios in the trade.

**Other common fume components** include iron oxide, zinc oxide (from galvanized steel — causes "metal fume fever," a nasty flu-like illness that hits hours after exposure), aluminum, nickel, and various shielding and flux gases.

**Ventilation is the primary control.** Outdoors with natural air movement, fumes usually disperse. Indoors or in enclosed spaces, you need mechanical ventilation — positioned to draw fumes *away* from your breathing zone, not across it. Local exhaust ventilation (LEV) with a fume extractor positioned near the arc is the gold standard for indoor work.

**Positioning matters.** Keep your head out of the plume. It sounds obvious, but welders naturally lean in to see the puddle — and the puddle is exactly where the fumes are. Learn to position yourself so you're upwind or above the work.

**Respirators.** When ventilation can't keep exposure below occupational limits, respiratory protection is required. A P100 half-face respirator handles particulates; welding stainless steel may require more. In confined spaces, supplied-air respirators are used. Whatever you use, it must be fit-tested and maintained. A respirator sitting in your truck doesn't protect you.

**The long game.** Fume damage is cumulative and silent. A welder who skips ventilation for twenty years doesn't feel it until it's too late. The professionals who last in this trade are the ones who took fumes seriously from day one.

## 1.5 Fire Prevention

Welding produces temperatures over 3,000°C (5,400°F) and throws sparks and spatter that can travel **10 metres (35 feet) or more**. Hot metal can smolder inside a wall cavity or under debris for *hours* before bursting into flame — which is why the fire rules exist the way they do.

**Hot work permits.** On professional sites, welding is "hot work" and requires a permit before you start. The permit confirms the area is clear of combustibles, that a fire watch is assigned, and that extinguishers are available. If your workplace has a hot work program, follow it to the letter — it's there because fires have started exactly the way it prevents.

**The 35-foot rule.** Remove all combustible materials within at least 35 feet (about 11 metres) of the welding area. Sweep the floor. Look up — combustible dust and materials on overhead surfaces count too. Anything that can't be moved gets covered with fire-resistant blankets.

**Fire watch.** A dedicated person whose only job is to watch for fire during welding *and for at least 30 to 60 minutes after welding stops* — the "30-minute rule" is the minimum; many standards require 60 minutes. The fire watch must have an extinguisher and know how to use it, plus a way to raise the alarm. This is not a role you combine with other work. Watching for fire *is* the work.

**Extinguishers.** Know which type you have and what it fights: ABC dry-chemical extinguishers handle ordinary combustibles, flammable liquids, and electrical fires — and they're the most common on welding sites. Know where the nearest extinguisher is before you strike an arc, not after you smell smoke.

**Never weld on:** containers that held flammable materials (even "empty" drums — residual vapors explode), near flammable liquid storage, or on structures coated with flammable paints or sealants without proper preparation.

## 1.6 Electrical Shock Prevention

The welder's equipment carries enough voltage and current to kill. The primary welding circuit (the wall side) and the secondary circuit (electrode to workpiece) both deserve respect.

**How welders get shocked:**
- Touching the electrode holder with wet gloves or wet clothing
- Working in wet conditions — rain, standing water, sweating through clothes
- Damaged cables or electrode holders exposing bare conductors
- Touching the workpiece and electrode simultaneously in the wrong conditions
- Open-circuit voltage: even when you're not welding, the machine maintains voltage across the leads

**Prevention, as rules to live by:**
- **Inspect cables daily.** Look for cracked insulation, exposed copper, loose connections. Damaged cable gets repaired or replaced — never taped over and ignored.
- **Keep yourself dry.** Dry gloves, dry clothes, dry boots, dry work surface. Wet = conductive = dangerous.
- **Don't coil the electrode cable around your body.** It feels convenient; it creates an induction loop and keeps live cable against your torso.
- **Ground the workpiece properly.** The work clamp goes on the work or the work table — clean metal-to-metal contact, not clamped onto painted or rusty surfaces.
- **Disconnect power when changing electrodes with bare hands** — actually, never change electrodes with bare hands. Use gloves, always.
- **In confined or damp spaces,** additional precautions apply: insulated mats, dry platforms, and often reduced open-circuit voltage equipment.

**Secondary circuit safety is a habit, not a one-time check.** Every morning: cables, holders, clamps, connections. Two minutes that have saved lives.

## 1.7 Safe Work Area Setup & Emergency Procedures

**Setting up your work area:**
- Clear the 35-foot zone of combustibles (see 1.5)
- Set up welding screens to protect passersby from arc flash
- Position ventilation to draw fumes away from your breathing zone
- Keep the floor dry and clear of trip hazards — cables, hoses, and tools are the biggest trip risks
- Ensure you can reach your extinguisher and that you know where the nearest fire alarm is
- Confirm your fire watch is in place and knows the plan

**Confined spaces deserve special attention.** Tanks, vessels, and enclosed compartments concentrate fumes, displace oxygen, and amplify fire and electrical risks. Confined space welding requires its own permit, atmospheric testing, a standby attendant, and often ventilation plus supplied air. If you haven't been trained in confined space entry, don't enter — full stop.

**Emergency procedures — know these before you need them:**
- **Fire:** Stop welding, cut power, alert others, use the extinguisher if it's safe to do so, evacuate if it's not. Never put yourself between a fire and your exit.
- **Electrical shock:** Do not touch the victim while they're in contact with the circuit — cut power first, then use a non-conductive object to separate them if needed. Call for emergency help immediately.
- **Arc eye injury:** Rest the eyes, avoid light, seek medical attention if pain is severe or vision is affected. Don't rub them.
- **Fume exposure symptoms** (dizziness, nausea, metal taste): get to fresh air immediately and seek medical attention.
- **Burns:** Cool with running water, cover, and get medical attention for anything beyond minor. Burns from welding can be deep — when in doubt, get checked.

**The final rule of this module:** no weld is worth an injury. If something looks unsafe — frayed cables, no ventilation, no fire watch, wet conditions — you stop. You fix it. You weld when it's safe. That is not optional, and any employer worth working for will back you on it.
