# Module 1: Electrical Safety Foundations

*Module 1 of the Home Electrical Basics course. Read/watch time: ~45 minutes.*

> **⚠️ DISCLAIMER — READ THIS FIRST:** This lesson is educational content only. It is not a substitute for a licensed electrician, and it does not qualify you to perform electrical work professionally. Electricity can kill — quickly, silently, and without a second chance. Everything in this module exists to keep you alive and unhurt. Always follow your local electrical codes (in Canada, the Canadian Electrical Code / CEC, plus your province's amendments), obtain required permits and inspections, and **when in doubt, call a licensed electrician.** No lesson is worth your life.

## Why This Module Comes First — And Why It Matters More Than Everything Else

Here's something you learn fast when you inspect electrical installations on construction sites: electricity doesn't care about your intentions. It doesn't care that you're "just changing an outlet real quick." It doesn't care that you've done it before. The electricians who get hurt are rarely the careless rookies — they're often experienced people who skipped one step, one time.

Every module in this course builds on this one. You will not touch a wire, open a box, or flip a breaker for a repair until the safety habits in this module are second nature. That's not us being dramatic. That's the job.

By the end of this module, you will understand how electricity actually works, the golden rules that keep people safe, how to work your breaker panel with confidence, what GFCI and AFCI protection do for you, what shock and arc flash really are — and exactly which jobs you should never, ever attempt yourself.

Let's begin.

## Part 1: How Electricity Works — Voltage, Current & Resistance in Plain Language

You don't need an engineering degree. You need three concepts and one analogy.

### The water-pipe analogy

Imagine water flowing through a pipe:
- **Voltage** is the water *pressure* — how hard the water is being pushed.
- **Current** is the *flow rate* — how much water is actually moving through the pipe.
- **Resistance** is anything *narrowing the pipe* — friction, a kink, a valve — that fights the flow.

Electricity behaves the same way. Voltage pushes, current flows, resistance opposes.

In your home:
- **Voltage** is measured in **volts (V)**. Standard Canadian household circuits run at **120 volts** (for outlets, lights, and small appliances) and **240 volts** (for stoves, dryers, and other heavy loads). That pressure is always there when the circuit is energized — whether anything is plugged in or not.
- **Current** is measured in **amperes**, or **amps (A)**. This is the actual flow of electricity. A phone charger draws less than 1 amp; a space heater can draw 12+ amps.
- **Resistance** is measured in **ohms (Ω)**. Every material resists current to some degree. Copper wire has very low resistance (that's why we use it). Your body has resistance too — but not enough to protect you.

### The critical fact: current is what hurts you

Here's the part that surprises most beginners: it's not the voltage that kills — it's the current flowing *through your body*. Voltage is what pushes that current through you, but the damage is done by the amps.

To put it in perspective:
- **1 milliamp (0.001 A):** barely perceptible tingle.
- **10–20 milliamps:** painful shock; your muscles may lock up so you *can't let go* of whatever you're touching.
- **50–100 milliamps across the chest:** can disrupt your heartbeat and cause cardiac arrest.
- **Above that:** severe burns, tissue damage, death.

A standard 15-amp household circuit can deliver **hundreds of times** the current needed to kill you. The breaker in your panel is there to protect the *wiring* from overheating — it is not fast enough or sensitive enough to protect *you* from shock. Never assume a breaker will save you. That's what the safety rules are for.

### One more concept: the circuit must be complete

Electricity needs a complete loop — a circuit — to flow. Power leaves the panel on the "hot" wire, flows through your device (a lamp, a toaster), and returns on the "neutral" wire. If you touch a live wire while you're grounded (standing on a concrete floor, touching a metal pipe, leaning on a grounded appliance), **your body becomes part of the circuit** — current flows through you to reach ground. That's a shock. That's why "test before you touch" isn't optional.

## Part 2: The Golden Safety Rules

These rules are non-negotiable. Not "usually." Not "when it's convenient." Every time, no exceptions. Professional electricians follow these rules on every job, and so will you.

### Rule 1: Always de-energize

**Before you open any electrical box, remove any cover plate, or touch any wire: turn the circuit OFF at the breaker panel.**

Not at the wall switch — at the breaker. A wall switch only interrupts one wire; the box can still be live. The breaker is your positive disconnect.

Then — and this is the part people skip — **tell everyone in the house what you're doing.** "I'm working on the kitchen outlets; the breaker labeled KITCHEN PLUGS is off. Do not touch the panel." On a job site, we'd use a physical lock on the breaker. At home, clear communication is your lockout.

### Rule 2: Test before you touch

Turning the breaker off is step one. **Verifying it's actually off is step two — and it's the step that saves lives.**

Breakers get mislabeled. Panels get confusing. Someone "helpfully" flips one back on. So before your fingers go near anything:

1. Use a **non-contact voltage tester** — hold it near the wires. No light, no beep = no voltage detected.
2. **Test the tester first** on a known-live outlet, so you know it's working. A dead tester gives false confidence.
3. Then test the wires in the box you're about to work on.

This takes thirty seconds. There is no repair so urgent that you skip the thirty seconds.

### Rule 3: Lockout basics for homeowners

On industrial and commercial sites, lockout/tagout (LOTO) is law: you physically lock the breaker in the OFF position and tag it with your name so nobody can re-energize it while you work. At home, you can buy an inexpensive **breaker lockout device** — a small clamp that fits over a breaker toggle. If you do your own electrical work more than once, get one. At minimum, tape over the breaker and leave a clear note: "DO NOT TURN ON — WORK IN PROGRESS."

### Rule 4: PPE — personal protective equipment

You don't need a full arc-flash suit for changing an outlet. But the basics matter:
- **Safety glasses** — always. Wire ends snap, debris falls from ceiling boxes, and you only get one pair of eyes.
- **Insulated tools** — screwdrivers and pliers rated for electrical work (look for the 1000V rating mark). They cost a little more than bargain-bin tools. Your hands are worth it.
- **Dry hands, dry environment** — never work on electrical with wet hands, standing water, or in damp conditions. Water dramatically lowers your body's resistance, which means dramatically more current through you for the same voltage.
- **One hand when possible** — old electrician's habit: keep one hand in your pocket or behind your back when near live parts. Current across your chest (hand to hand) is far more dangerous than current through one hand to ground. You'll rarely be near live parts if you follow Rule 1 — but the habit is worth building.

## Part 3: Understanding Your Breaker Panel

Your breaker panel (also called a load center or distribution panel) is the heart of your home's electrical system. Every circuit in your house starts here. Knowing your panel isn't just useful — it's essential.

### What's inside

- **Main breaker:** The big breaker at the top (usually). It shuts off power to *the entire panel* — every circuit at once. Know where it is. In an emergency (smell of burning, sparking), this is what you switch off.
- **Branch circuit breakers:** The rows of smaller breakers. Each one protects one circuit — a set of outlets, a lighting run, a dedicated appliance circuit. They're typically rated 15 amps (general outlets and lighting) or 20 amps (kitchen counter outlets, some dedicated circuits), with larger ones for stoves, dryers, and air conditioners.
- **The dead front (cover):** The metal cover with the breaker toggles poking through. Behind it are the bus bars — live metal that distributes power to every breaker. **You never remove this cover.** What's behind it stays the domain of licensed electricians.

### Reading breaker labels

Open your panel door and look at the directory — the label listing which breaker controls what. If yours says "plugs," "lights," and a bunch of blanks... you're not alone. Most are terrible. **Fix yours.** Spend an afternoon with a helper: turn circuits off one at a time, note what loses power, and write clear labels. ("Kitchen counter outlets — east wall" beats "plugs" every time.) A well-labeled panel is a safety device. In an emergency, you don't want to guess.

### How to safely switch a breaker

1. Stand to the side of the panel, not directly in front of it. (In the extremely unlikely event of an arc flash, you don't want to be in the direct path.)
2. Flip the breaker firmly to OFF. It should stay in the OFF position.
3. If a breaker **won't stay off, feels hot, smells like burning, or shows scorch marks** — stop. Do not force it. That's a "call an electrician" situation.
4. To re-energize: flip firmly to ON. Stand to the side here too.

### What you don't touch

The main breaker exists so *you* can kill power to the whole house in an emergency. Beyond that, everything inside the panel — the bus bars, the main lugs, the wiring behind the dead front — is **off-limits for DIY**. The service wires feeding your panel are always live, even with the main breaker off, and they carry enough current to kill instantly. Panel interiors are electrician territory. Full stop.

## Part 4: GFCI & AFCI — What They Are and Why They Matter

Your breakers protect wires. **GFCI and AFCI devices protect people and homes.** Know the difference — it could save a life.

### GFCI — Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter

A GFCI watches the current going out on the hot wire and coming back on the neutral wire. If even a tiny amount — as little as 5 milliamps — goes missing (leaking through *you* to ground, for example), it trips in a fraction of a second, cutting power before the shock becomes lethal.

**Where you'll find them:** Bathrooms, kitchens (counter outlets), garages, outdoors, basements, laundry areas — anywhere water and electricity might meet. They look like outlets with "TEST" and "RESET" buttons, or as special breakers in your panel.

**Test them monthly.** Press TEST — it should trip with a click. Press RESET — power should restore. If a GFCI won't test or won't reset, replace it (that's a Module 3 skill) or call an electrician. A dead GFCI is a silent hazard.

### AFCI — Arc Fault Circuit Interrupter

An AFCI listens for the electrical signature of *arcing* — dangerous sparking inside walls from damaged wires, loose connections, or chewed insulation. Arcing generates intense heat and is a leading cause of electrical fires. The AFCI trips before that heat can ignite surrounding material.

**Where you'll find them:** Bedrooms and living areas in modern homes (required by current Canadian code for most living-space circuits). They look like breakers with a TEST button in your panel.

**Why it matters to you:** If an AFCI breaker keeps tripping, don't just reset it and move on — it's telling you something. It could be a nuisance trip from a particular device, or it could be a genuine arc fault in your walls. If it trips repeatedly with nothing plugged in, call an electrician.

## Part 5: Shock & Arc-Flash Awareness — and What You Should NEVER DIY

### How shocks happen

Almost every shock follows the same script: someone touched something they believed was dead, and it wasn't. The causes are depressingly consistent:
- Skipped the voltage test ("I turned the breaker off, it's fine")
- Wrong breaker (mislabeled panel)
- Backfed or multi-wire circuits (more than one circuit in the same box — the other one is still live)
- Damaged insulation exposing live conductors
- Water where it shouldn't be

Every one of these is preventable by the golden rules. De-energize. Test. Verify. Every time.

### What arc flash is — and why professionals fear it

An **arc flash** is an explosive release of energy caused by an electrical short circuit — electricity arcing through the air between conductors. Temperatures can exceed the surface of the sun. It produces a blinding flash, a pressure wave, and molten metal spray. Arc flash is the reason electricians wear rated PPE and stand to the side when operating breakers, and it's the reason **you never open a panel's dead front or work on anything upstream of your branch breakers.**

For basic homeowner repairs done correctly — power off, tested dead — arc flash is not a realistic concern. It becomes a concern when people open panels, work live, or mess with service equipment. Don't be that person.

### ⛔ What you should NEVER DIY

Memorize this list. If a job falls on it, you call a licensed electrician. No exceptions, no "but it looks simple":

1. **Anything inside the breaker panel** — behind the dead front, on the bus bars, on the main lugs. The service feed is always live and lethal.
2. **Service entrance and meter work** — the wires from the street to your house, the meter base, the main disconnect. Utility and electrician territory only.
3. **Aluminum wiring repairs** — homes wired with aluminum (common in the 1960s–70s) need special techniques and materials. Aluminum expands, contracts, and oxidizes differently than copper, and improper connections cause fires. This is specialist work.
4. **Adding new circuits or upgrading service** — new home runs, subpanels, service upgrades. These require permits, inspections, and code expertise.
5. **Anything involving the main breaker connections or service mast.**
6. **Knob-and-tube wiring** — if your older home still has it, don't touch it. Get it assessed by a professional.
7. **Anything you're unsure about.** This is the most important rule on the list. Uncertainty is your brain's warning system. A $150 service call is infinitely cheaper than a hospital bill — or worse.

### The inspector's mindset

Here's what years of construction QA teach you: the best electricians aren't the fastest or the most confident. They're the most *methodical*. They verify instead of assume. They test instead of trust. They walk away from anything outside their scope.

Adopt that mindset and you'll be safer than most DIYers on day one. The goal of this course isn't to make you fearless around electricity — it's to make you **competent, careful, and honest about your limits.** That combination is what keeps people safe.
