# Module 1: Safety Mindset & PPE

**DISCLAIMER: This course provides safety AWARENESS education. It is NOT a substitute for certified safety training (e.g., CSTS, OSSA, or site-specific orientations). Always follow your employer's safety program and your province's Occupational Health & Safety legislation.**

---

## 1.1 The Real Cost of Incidents

Construction work builds everything society stands on — and it carries risks that office work simply doesn't. Workers fall from heights, get struck by materials and equipment, are caught in machinery and collapsing trenches, and are electrocuted by temporary power and overhead lines. These aren't rare freak events; they are the everyday hazards of the industry, and they injure and kill workers every year across Canada.

The human cost is the one that matters. An injured worker doesn't just miss shifts — they face pain, surgeries, rehabilitation, and sometimes a career and a life permanently changed. Their family absorbs it too: lost income, stress, and a future that looks nothing like what was planned. No project schedule, no bonus, no deadline is worth that. It never has been.

The financial cost is real as well, and it's worth understanding even if you're not the one paying. A serious workplace incident can cost an employer hundreds of thousands of dollars — medical and compensation costs, work stoppages, investigations, fines from regulators, and increased insurance premiums for years. A small contractor can be bankrupted by a single severe incident. And for the injured worker, the financial hit — even with compensation systems — is devastating: lost wages, uncovered expenses, and a recovery that doesn't fit into anyone's budget.

Here is the point of this framing: **incidents are expensive for everyone and preventable by almost everyone.** The vast majority of construction incidents are the result of hazards that were visible before the injury happened. This course is about learning to see them.

## 1.2 Safety Culture vs. Compliance

There are two ways a site handles safety. The first is compliance: rules exist on paper, orientations get signed, inspections get passed — and underneath, everyone cuts corners the moment nobody's watching. Compliance keeps a site legal. It does not keep people safe.

The second is culture: safety is how the crew actually works, every day, whether the inspector is present or not. In a strong safety culture, workers look out for each other, speak up about hazards, and treat shortcuts as unacceptable — not because a rule says so, but because it's what the crew does.

You can tell the difference quickly:
- **Compliance site:** PPE worn only when supervisors are near. Hazards reported rarely. "Don't rock the boat" is the unwritten rule.
- **Culture site:** PPE worn because it's habit. Anyone can call a stop to unsafe work. Near misses get reported and discussed. The newest apprentice can tell the foreman something looks wrong — and gets thanked for it.

Here's the honest truth: most sites sit somewhere in between. And **you** get to decide which direction you push it. You don't need authority to refuse unsafe work, report a hazard, or wear your PPE properly. Culture is built by individuals making the safe choice repeatedly until it becomes the norm. That can start with you, on your first day.

## 1.3 PPE Deep Dive

Personal protective equipment is your last line of defense — the thing that protects you when every other control has failed. It's also the most visible sign of whether someone takes safety seriously. Here's the full kit, head to toe, with what actually matters about each item:

**Hard hats.** Protect against falling objects, head strikes, and — with the right type — electrical contact. Your hard hat should meet CSA Z94.1 (the Canadian standard) or equivalent. **Fit:** the suspension should hold it firmly without rocking; the chin strap matters when working at height or in wind. **Inspection:** check the shell for cracks, dents, and UV damage (a sun-bleached, chalky shell is weakened). **Replacement:** hard hats have a service life — typically 5 years from manufacture for the shell, and the suspension wears out faster. A hard hat that took a real impact gets replaced immediately, even if it looks fine.

**Eye and face protection.** Safety glasses with side shields are the minimum on most sites. Goggles seal against dust and chemical splash. Face shields add protection for grinding and cutting — but a face shield is worn *over* safety glasses, never instead of them. **Fit:** no gaps at the temples, no slipping when you bend over. **Inspection:** scratched lenses get replaced — workers who can't see clearly through scratched glasses take them off, which defeats the purpose entirely.

**Hearing protection.** Construction noise — heavy equipment, pile drivers, grinders, nail guns — routinely exceeds safe levels. Foam earplugs, banded plugs, or earmuffs all work if worn correctly. **Fit:** foam plugs must be rolled, inserted, and allowed to expand — a plug sitting halfway out is doing almost nothing. **Inspection/replacement:** plugs are disposable; earmuff cushions wear out and crack. Rule of thumb: if you have to shout to be heard by someone an arm's length away, you need hearing protection.

**Respiratory protection.** Dust, silica, fumes, and vapours are the invisible hazards of construction. The right protection ranges from a simple N95 disposable mask for general dust to half-face respirators with the correct cartridges for specific contaminants (silica, lead paint dust, welding fumes). **Fit:** this is everything. A respirator that doesn't seal is a false sense of security. Facial hair breaks the seal — this is why clean-shaven policies exist where respirators are required. **Inspection:** check straps, valves, and cartridges; replace cartridges on schedule, not when you start smelling things.

**Gloves.** The right glove depends on the task: cut-resistant for sharp materials, leather for rough handling, chemical-resistant for solvents and concrete (wet concrete causes serious chemical burns — this surprises people), insulated for cold. **Fit:** gloves that are too big get caught in machinery; too small and you won't wear them. **Inspection:** worn-through palms, holes, and chemical damage mean replacement.

**High-visibility clothing.** On any site with equipment traffic, hi-vis is non-negotiable. CSA-compliant hi-vis (typically Class 2 or 3 with reflective striping) keeps you visible to equipment operators. **Fit:** it must be worn *over* outer layers — hi-vis buried under a jacket helps nobody. **Inspection:** faded fabric and peeling reflective tape reduce visibility; replace when the colour dulls.

**Safety boots.** CSA-approved (green triangle) boots with puncture-resistant soles and metatarsal protection where required. **Fit:** broken-in but supportive; blisters mean you'll avoid wearing them. **Inspection:** worn treads lose slip resistance; cracked leather loses protection; separated soles are a trip and puncture hazard. Laces tied and tucked — loose laces on a construction site are a trip hazard and a spark catch.

**The golden rule of PPE: it's only protective if it's worn, worn correctly, and in good condition.** A hard hat in your truck, glasses in your pocket, and a respirator with a broken strap are just expensive decorations.

## 1.4 PPE Inspection Routines

Inspection isn't a monthly event — it's a daily habit that takes two minutes. Here's a routine that works:

**The morning check (before work starts):**
1. **Hard hat:** shell — no cracks, dents, or deep scratches; suspension — intact, adjusted; check the manufacture date.
2. **Eyes:** lenses clean and unscratched; frames intact; side shields in place.
3. **Hearing:** plugs fresh or earmuff cushions pliable and uncracked.
4. **Respirator (if required):** straps elastic, valves clean, cartridges within service date; do a seal check every time you put it on.
5. **Gloves:** no holes, thin spots, or chemical damage; right type for today's task.
6. **Hi-vis:** colour bright, reflective tape intact, worn as the outer layer.
7. **Boots:** treads intact, leather uncracked, laces tied and tucked, toe caps undamaged.

**During the day:** If your PPE takes a hit — a dropped tool on your hard hat, a splash, a tear — stop and assess. Damaged PPE comes off and gets replaced, not "finished the shift with."

**The replacement mindset:** PPE is consumable. Workers who treat a $15 pair of glasses as too precious to replace are risking eyes worth infinitely more. Employers are required to provide PPE in most cases — and where they are, there's no excuse for wearing damaged gear. If your employer won't replace damaged PPE, that's a conversation to have with your safety representative — and a red flag about the site's culture.

## 1.5 Common PPE Mistakes Seen on Sites

After years on construction sites, the same mistakes show up everywhere. Recognize them in yourself and your crew:

1. **Hard hat backwards.** Some hats are designed to be worn reversed; most are not. A backwards non-reversible hat loses its brim protection and suspension geometry. Check the manufacturer's design.
2. **Safety glasses on the forehead.** The most common sight on any site. If they fog up, get anti-fog lenses — don't abandon them.
3. **Earplugs hanging around the neck.** Plugs protect ears, not necks. If they're uncomfortable, try a different type — comfort is a fit problem, not a reason to skip protection.
4. **Hi-vis under the jacket.** Invisible is invisible. Hi-vis goes on the outside, always.
5. **Welding or cutting without the right eye protection.** Sunglasses are not safety glasses. Regular prescription glasses are not impact-rated. Grinding sparks will find unprotected eyes.
6. **Gloves off "just for a second."** The second you take gloves off to handle a sharp edge or hot material is the second you get cut or burned. Keep them on.
7. **Respirator with a beard.** Facial hair breaks the seal. If the job requires a tight-fitting respirator, the beard goes or the job assignment changes. This isn't a preference — it's physics.
8. **Worn-out boots kept "because they're comfortable."** Comfort that comes from broken-down structure is a hazard. Replace them.

Every one of these has caused real injuries. Every one of them is a choice.

## 1.6 Your Rights — Know, Participate, Refuse

In Canada, occupational health and safety legislation gives every worker three fundamental rights. These apply in every province and territory, though the exact legislation name varies (e.g., Alberta's OHS Act, Ontario's OHSA, BC's Workers Compensation Act). Know them:

**1. The right to know.** You have the right to know about the hazards in your workplace — what you're exposed to, what the risks are, and how to protect yourself. This is why orientations, safety data sheets, hazard assessments, and toolbox talks exist. If nobody has told you about the hazards of your task, ask. "Nobody told me" is never an acceptable reason for an injury.

**2. The right to participate.** You have the right to take part in workplace health and safety — through joint health and safety committees, as a safety representative, or simply by raising concerns and suggesting improvements. Your experience doing the actual work makes your input valuable. Sites with active worker participation are measurably safer.

**3. The right to refuse unsafe work.** This is the most powerful right you have, and the one workers are most afraid to use. If you have reasonable grounds to believe your work is dangerous to you or others, **you can refuse to do it.** The process: tell your supervisor immediately, explain the danger, and the employer must investigate. You cannot be disciplined or fired for exercising this right in good faith — that's reprisal, and it's illegal.

Using the right to refuse isn't dramatic. It sounds like: *"I'm not comfortable doing this until the guardrail is up"* or *"This trench doesn't look shored — I need to stop until it's checked."* Professional, specific, and firm. Any employer who punishes you for that is telling you exactly what kind of employer they are.

**New workers, listen carefully:** you are at the highest risk of injury in your first weeks on a job — unfamiliar tasks, unfamiliar site, and the pressure to prove yourself. That pressure is exactly when your rights matter most. No job is worth getting hurt on your first week, or any week. Ask the questions. Wear the PPE. Refuse the unsafe task. The good employers will respect you more for it, not less.
