# Module 1: How Heating & Cooling Works

*Read this entire module before touching anything on your system. Understanding comes first; wrenches come later (and mostly belong to the technician).*

---

## Lesson 1: The Heating Cycle, Step by Step

Let's follow a single "call for heat" from start to finish. This is the story of every forced-air furnace cycle, and once you can tell it, you understand 80% of residential heating.

**Step 1: The thermostat calls for heat.** Your thermostat is constantly comparing the room temperature to your setpoint. When the room drops a degree or two below the setpoint, the thermostat closes a low-voltage circuit — essentially sending an electrical "wake up" signal to the furnace. That's all a thermostat does: it's a temperature-sensitive switch. (Smart features aside, the core job hasn't changed in a hundred years.)

**Step 2: Ignition and the burner.** The furnace receives the call and runs a short safety sequence first — modern furnaces check that the exhaust venting is clear (via a pressure switch) before they'll light anything. Then the igniter glows (like a tiny electric stove element) or a pilot lights, the gas valve opens, and the burners ignite. Blue flames heat up.

**Step 3: The heat exchanger warms up.** The burners don't heat your air directly — that would mix combustion gases with your breathing air, which is exactly as bad as it sounds. Instead, the flames heat a **heat exchanger**: a set of metal chambers or tubes. The combustion gases stay *inside* the exchanger and exit through the flue; your household air flows *around the outside* of it and picks up the heat through the metal. The heat exchanger is the wall between "fire" and "your lungs." Remember this — it matters enormously in Lesson 5.

**Step 4: The blower kicks in.** Once the heat exchanger is hot enough (there's a delay — usually 30 to 60 seconds — so the system doesn't blow cold air at you), the **blower motor** starts. It's a big fan that pushes household air across the hot heat exchanger and into the ductwork.

**Step 5: Ducts deliver, returns collect.** **Supply ducts** carry the warmed air to registers in each room. Meanwhile, **return ducts** pull cooler room air back to the furnace to be reheated. It's a loop — the same air circulating, getting a heat top-up each pass. This is why blocked return vents are such a problem: choke the return, and you choke the whole cycle.

**Step 6: The thermostat is satisfied.** Room temperature reaches the setpoint, the thermostat opens the circuit, the gas valve closes, the burners shut off — and the blower keeps running for a minute or two to squeeze the remaining heat out of the exchanger (this cool-down also protects the heat exchanger from thermal stress).

That's one cycle. On a cold Alberta night, your furnace might run dozens of cycles. Every component you just met — thermostat, igniter, gas valve, burners, heat exchanger, blower, ductwork — has a job, a failure mode, and a place on the "yours vs. the technician's" line. We'll map all of that in Lesson 4.

## Lesson 2: Furnaces vs. Boilers vs. Heat Pumps

Not every home heats the same way. Here's how to tell what you have and how it differs.

**Forced-air furnace (the most common in Canada).** Burns natural gas, propane, or oil (or uses electric heating elements) to heat air, then blows it through ducts. You have one if you have floor or wall registers/vents blowing warm air. Pros: also powers your air conditioning through the same ducts (one distribution system, two jobs); fast to warm a house; filter in one place. Cons: can dry the air; ductwork takes space; you're heating air, which is a mediocre carrier of heat compared to water.

**Boiler (hydronic heating).** Heats *water*, then circulates it through radiators, baseboards, or in-floor tubing. You have one if you have radiators or baseboard units with pipes (not electric baseboards — those are different) and no ductwork blowing air. Pros: comfortable, even, quiet heat; no dust blown around; in-floor radiant is luxurious. Cons: can't do air conditioning through the same system; slower to respond to thermostat changes; leaks mean water damage, not just a service call.

**Heat pump.** Doesn't *create* heat — it *moves* it, using the refrigeration cycle in reverse (more on this in Lesson 3). In winter it extracts heat from outdoor air (yes, even cold air contains heat energy) and pumps it inside; in summer it runs the other way as an air conditioner. Pros: extremely efficient in mild weather (it moves 2–4 units of heat for every unit of electricity); one unit heats and cools. Cons: efficiency drops as it gets very cold — which is why in Alberta, heat pumps are usually paired with a gas furnace backup (a "dual fuel" or "hybrid" system) that takes over below roughly −15 to −25°C, depending on the unit.

**How to identify yours:** Look at the big metal box in your basement or utility room. A furnace has ductwork (big rectangular metal trunks) attached. A boiler has pipes and often a small expansion tank, with no large ducts. A heat pump has an outdoor unit that runs in winter (unlike a straight AC condenser, which sits idle all winter). The data plate on the unit — usually a sticker with the model number — will also name the type and fuel.

## Lesson 3: How Air Conditioning Works — The Refrigeration Cycle, Simply

Air conditioners don't create cold. They **move heat** — from inside your house to outside. The refrigeration cycle is the same physics in your fridge, your AC, and a heat pump in cooling mode. Here's the plain-language version:

**The sponge-and-bucket analogy.** Imagine a sponge that soaks up heat instead of water. You soak it up indoors (it absorbs heat from your house air), wring it out outdoors (it dumps the heat outside), and repeat. The "sponge" is a chemical called **refrigerant**, and it circulates in a sealed loop through four stations:

1. **Evaporator coil (indoors).** Cold, low-pressure refrigerant flows through this coil while your blower pushes warm house air across it. The refrigerant *absorbs* heat from the air — soaking up the sponge. The air, now cooler (and drier — moisture condenses on the cold coil and drains away through the condensate line), goes back into your ducts.

2. **Compressor (outdoors).** The now-warm refrigerant vapor travels outside to the compressor, which squeezes it. Compressing a gas makes it hot — much hotter than the outdoor air. (This is the humming box beside your house, and the part that uses the most electricity.)

3. **Condenser coil (outdoors).** The super-hot, high-pressure refrigerant flows through the outdoor coil while a fan blows outdoor air across it. Because the refrigerant is hotter than the outside air, heat flows *out* of it — wringing out the sponge. The refrigerant cools and condenses back to a liquid.

4. **Expansion valve.** The liquid refrigerant passes through a tiny metering device that drops its pressure dramatically — and dropping pressure drops temperature. It enters the indoor evaporator cold again, ready to soak up more heat.

Round and round, all summer. A heat pump in winter just runs this loop *backwards*: the outdoor coil becomes the evaporator (soaking up heat from cold outdoor air) and the indoor coil becomes the condenser (dumping that heat into your house).

**What this means for you as a homeowner:** The refrigerant loop is **sealed and pressurized**. You never add refrigerant, check refrigerant, or open any part of this loop — that's licensed-technician work involving regulated chemicals and specialized gauges. Your job is everything *around* the loop: filters, airflow, keeping the outdoor unit clear, and noticing when cooling performance drops (which is often an airflow problem you *can* fix, not a refrigerant problem).

## Lesson 4: Key Components and What Goes Wrong With Each

Meet the cast, with each part's typical failure mode and whose problem it is.

| Component | What it does | What goes wrong | Yours or the tech's? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Thermostat** | Temperature-sensitive switch; calls for heat/cool | Dead batteries, wrong mode, miscalibration, bad location | **Yours** — check settings, batteries, and placement first, always |
| **Air filter** | Traps dust before it hits the equipment | Clogs → restricts airflow → overheating, shutdowns, frozen AC coils | **Yours** — the single most important homeowner task |
| **Igniter / pilot** | Lights the burners | Wears out (hot-surface igniters are a consumable); pilot goes out | **Tech's** — inside the combustion area |
| **Burners** | Burn gas to make heat | Sooting, misalignment, delayed ignition | **Tech's** — combustion work, always |
| **Heat exchanger** | Transfers burner heat to household air; keeps combustion gases separate | Cracks with age/thermal stress → **carbon monoxide can enter your air supply** | **Tech's** — and the reason for CO detectors (Lesson 6) |
| **Blower motor** | Pushes air through the system | Bearings wear, capacitor fails → weak or no airflow | **Tech's** — electrical + mechanical, inside the unit |
| **Ductwork** | Delivers conditioned air; returns room air | Leaks, disconnections, crushed flex duct → lost efficiency, uneven rooms | **Yours** for obvious disconnected joints in accessible areas; **tech's** for the rest |
| **Condensate drain** | Carries away moisture from the AC evaporator (and high-efficiency furnaces) | Clogs with algae → water backup, shutdowns, ceiling damage | **Yours** — keeping it clear is homeowner maintenance (Module 2) |
| **Outdoor condenser** | Dumps heat outside (AC / heat pump) | Blocked by vegetation, debris, snow → poor cooling, overheating | **Yours** — keep it clear; **tech's** for anything inside it |
| **Flue / venting** | Exhausts combustion gases outside | Blockage (bird nests, ice, snow) → furnace shuts down on safety switch — or worse | **Yours** to keep the exterior termination clear; **tech's** for the venting itself |
| **Refrigerant lines** | Sealed loop carrying refrigerant | Leaks (rare, but they happen) → weak cooling | **Tech's** — always; regulated chemicals |

**The pattern:** If it's a setting, a filter, a vent you can see, a battery, or a blockage you can reach without opening the equipment — it's yours. If it's inside the cabinet, involves gas, combustion, high voltage, or refrigerant — it's the technician's. There is no grey area worth exploring.

## Lesson 5: Efficiency Ratings Explained — AFUE and SEER

Those stickers on your equipment are telling you how much of your money becomes heat (or cooling) versus waste. Here's how to read them.

**AFUE — Annual Fuel Utilization Efficiency (heating).** This is the percentage of fuel your furnace or boiler converts into usable heat over a heating season. An 80% AFUE furnace turns 80 cents of every gas dollar into heat and wastes 20 cents up the flue. A 96% AFUE furnace wastes only 4 cents. What the numbers mean in practice:

- **80% AFUE:** Older standard-efficiency. If your furnace is 20+ years old, it's probably here. It has a metal flue and a standing pilot or simple ignition.
- **90–98% AFUE:** Modern high-efficiency (condensing) furnaces. They extract so much heat that the exhaust is cool enough to vent through plastic PVC pipe out the side of your house — that's why you see those white pipes on newer homes. They also produce condensate water (hence the condensate drain in the table above).

**The dollar math:** Going from 80% to 96% AFUE cuts the *wasted* portion of your heating fuel by 80% (from 20% waste to 4%). On a $2,000/year heating bill, that's roughly $320/year back in your pocket — which is why a new high-efficiency furnace often pays for itself well within its lifespan, especially in a climate like Alberta's.

**SEER — Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio (cooling).** The cooling equivalent: total cooling output over a season divided by electricity input. Higher is better. Older units might be SEER 10–13; modern minimums in Canada are higher, and premium units reach into the 20s. Unlike AFUE (a percentage), SEER is a ratio — but the direction is the same: bigger number, lower bills.

**HSPF** is the heating-season equivalent for heat pumps — you'll see it if you're shopping for one.

**What to do with this:** Find your equipment's ratings on the data plate or the original paperwork. Write them down. When a technician tells you your 22-year-old 80% furnace "still has life in it," you'll be able to do your own math on what that life is costing you in wasted fuel every winter. Replacement decisions are dollars-and-sense decisions, and now you have the sense part.

## Lesson 6: Carbon Monoxide Safety — Serious, Not Scary

Let's talk about carbon monoxide (CO) plainly: what it is, why it matters, and the simple things that keep you safe. No fear-mongering — just facts and actions.

**What it is.** Carbon monoxide is a gas produced when fuel (natural gas, propane, oil, wood) burns *incompletely*. Your furnace, boiler, water heater, fireplace, and gas stove can all produce small amounts. Normally, it's vented safely outside through the flue. It becomes dangerous when venting fails or combustion goes wrong — a blocked flue, a cracked heat exchanger, a malfunctioning burner.

**Why it's dangerous.** CO is **colorless and odorless** — you can't see it or smell it. It binds to your blood's hemoglobin far more readily than oxygen does, effectively starving your body of oxygen. Early symptoms — headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion — are easy to dismiss as flu. That's what makes it insidious: by the time you feel seriously ill, your judgment is already impaired.

**The simple protections (do all of these):**

1. **CO detectors on every floor**, including the basement and near (not in) sleeping areas. Not "one in the hallway" — every floor. They're $30–50 CAD each. This is the cheapest life-safety device in your home.
2. **Test them monthly** (the test button) and **replace them per the manufacturer's schedule** — usually every 7–10 years. Write the install date on the unit with a marker.
3. **Never ignore an alarm.** If a CO detector alarms: get everyone (including pets) out of the house immediately, call 911 or your gas utility's emergency line from outside, and do not re-enter until professionals say it's safe. Not "open a window and see" — *out, then call*.
4. **Annual professional servicing** of fuel-burning appliances. A technician checks combustion, venting, and the heat exchanger — the three things that stand between normal operation and CO risk. This is the single best money you spend on your HVAC system each year.
5. **Keep exterior vents clear.** High-efficiency furnaces and water heaters vent through those PVC pipes on the side of your house — in an Alberta winter, drifting snow and ice can block them. A blocked intake/exhaust will usually trip the furnace's safety switch and shut it down (inconvenient), but venting problems should never be assumed benign. Check them after heavy snowfalls.

**The reassuring truth:** Millions of homes heat safely with gas every winter. CO incidents are rare *because* the safety layers work — proper installation, annual servicing, clear venting, and working detectors. You're not adding fear to your life by learning this; you're adding the layer that makes the rare event survivable. Detectors on every floor, tested monthly, alarm means out. That's the whole system, and now it's yours.

## Module 1 Practice Exercises

1. **Trace a heating cycle out loud.** Thermostat call → ignition → heat exchanger → blower → supply ducts → rooms → return ducts → thermostat satisfied. Say it until it's boring — that's when you know it.
2. **Identify your system.** Furnace, boiler, or heat pump? Find the data plate and write down the type, fuel, and AFUE (or equivalent) rating.
3. **Find your filter.** Where is it? What size? What MERV rating? When was it last changed? (If you can't answer all four, Module 2 is going to be very useful.)
4. **Locate your exterior intake/exhaust vents.** Walk outside and find them. Are they clear right now? You'll check them after every heavy snowfall from now on.
5. **Audit your CO detectors.** How many? What floors? When were they installed? Test every one today, and replace any past their service life.
6. **Find your furnace's power switch and breaker.** (Usually a light-switch-style box on or near the furnace, plus a labeled breaker in the panel.) Knowing these in advance is what makes the Module 4 "no heat" checklist fast instead of frantic.
