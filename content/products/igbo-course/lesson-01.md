# Module 2, Lesson 2.1–2.8 (Starter Lesson): Greetings & Introductions

Welcome! In Igbo culture, greeting is not small talk — it's the foundation of respect and relationship. A proper greeting can open doors that money can't. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to greet anyone at any time of day, introduce yourself, say please and thank you, and say goodbye warmly.

**A note on tones:** Igbo is a tonal language. High tone is marked ´ , low tone is marked ` . Don't stress about perfection today — just listen for whether the voice goes *up* or *down*, and imitate it. Your ear will sharpen fast.

---

## Part 1 — 12 Essential Greetings & Introductions

### 1. Ndewo
- **Pronunciation:** nn-deh-WOH (high tone on *wo*)
- **Meaning:** Hello (general greeting)
- **Usage:** Your safest, most universal greeting. Works anytime, anywhere, with anyone. Say it with a smile and you'll never go wrong.

### 2. Ụtụtụ ọma
- **Pronunciation:** oo-TOO-too oh-MAH (ụtụtụ: high-low-low; ọma: high)
- **Meaning:** Good morning
- **Usage:** Use from waking until around noon. Elders especially appreciate the full greeting rather than a casual "Ndewo" in the morning.

### 3. Ehihie ọma
- **Pronunciation:** eh-hee-HEE-eh oh-MAH
- **Meaning:** Good afternoon
- **Usage:** Midday to late afternoon. *Ehihie* is the afternoon sun — you're literally wishing someone "good afternoon sun."

### 4. Mgbede ọma
- **Pronunciation:** mm-beh-DEH oh-MAH
- **Meaning:** Good evening
- **Usage:** Evening greeting. (You'll also hear *Ụlahụ ọma* in some areas — same meaning, regional flavor.)

### 5. Kedu?
- **Pronunciation:** keh-DOO (rising tone)
- **Meaning:** How are you? / What's up? / How is it?
- **Usage:** The workhorse of Igbo conversation. Short, warm, endlessly useful. Often answered with *Ọ dị mma* (it's fine).

### 6. Kedu ka ị mere?
- **Pronunciation:** keh-DOO kah ee MEH-reh
- **Meaning:** How are you? (fuller, warmer form)
- **Usage:** Slightly more caring than a quick *Kedu?* — good for people you haven't seen in a while. Literally "how did you do?"

### 7. Aha m bụ ___
- **Pronunciation:** AH-hah mm boo
- **Meaning:** My name is ___
- **Usage:** Fill in your name: *Aha m bụ Adaeze.* The single most empowering sentence in this lesson — say it out loud right now.

### 8. Kedu aha gị?
- **Pronunciation:** keh-DOO AH-hah gee
- **Meaning:** What is your name?
- **Usage:** The polite way to ask someone's name. (*Gị* = "your.")

### 9. Biko
- **Pronunciation:** BEE-koh (high-high)
- **Meaning:** Please
- **Usage:** Use it liberally — before requests, to get attention politely, even to say "excuse me." A *biko* softens everything.

### 10. Daalụ
- **Pronunciation:** dah-ah-LOO (low-low-high)
- **Meaning:** Thank you
- **Usage:** Say it often and mean it. For extra warmth: *Daalụ nke ukwuu* (thank you very much) or *Daalu rinne* (thanks a lot).

### 11. Nnọọ
- **Pronunciation:** nn-noh-OH (rising at the end)
- **Meaning:** Welcome!
- **Usage:** Said to someone arriving — at your home, at an event, back from a journey. If someone says *Nnọọ* to you, smile big: you're being honored.

### 12. Ka ọ dị
- **Pronunciation:** kah oh DEE
- **Meaning:** Goodbye (warm farewell)
- **Usage:** Literally something like "until it is (well)" — a gentle, hopeful send-off. Much warmer than just walking away.

**Bonus phrases to sprinkle in:**
- **Ọ dị mma** (oh dee mmah) — It's fine / I'm fine / It's good. The universal positive answer.
- **Jisie ike** (jee-SEE-see ee-KEH) — Well done! / Keep it up! Say it to encourage someone.

## Part 2 — Three Mini-Dialogues

### Dialogue 1: Morning greeting with an elder
> **You:** Ụtụtụ ọma, Mazi Okoro! *(Good morning, Mr. Okoro!)*
>
> **Mazi Okoro:** Ụtụtụ ọma, nwa m. Kedu ka ị mere? *(Good morning, my child. How are you?)*
>
> **You:** Ọ dị mma, daalụ. Kedu ka ị mere? *(I'm fine, thank you. How are you?)*
>
> **Mazi Okoro:** Ọ dị mma. Jisie ike! *(I'm fine. Keep it up!)*

*Notice: you mirrored his greeting, asked after him in return, and he blessed your effort with "Jisie ike." That's a perfect exchange.*

### Dialogue 2: Introducing yourself at a gathering
> **Adaeze:** Ndewo! Aha m bụ Adaeze. Kedu aha gị? *(Hello! My name is Adaeze. What is your name?)*
>
> **You:** Ndewo, Adaeze! Aha m bụ ___ . Ọ dị m ụtọ izute gị. *(Hello, Adaeze! My name is ___. Pleased to meet you.)*
>
> **Adaeze:** Nnọọ! *(Welcome!)*
>
> **You:** Daalụ nke ukwuu! *(Thank you very much!)*

*New phrase: **Ọ dị m ụtọ izute gị** (oh dee mm oo-TOH ee-ZOO-teh gee) = "I'm pleased to meet you." A lovely formal touch.*

### Dialogue 3: Arriving at someone's home
> **You:** *(knocking)* Ndewo! *(Hello!)*
>
> **Host:** Nnọọ! Biko, bata. *(Welcome! Please, come in.)*
>
> **You:** Daalụ! Kedu? *(Thank you! How are things?)*
>
> **Host:** Ọ dị mma. *(All good.)*
>
> *(later, leaving)*
>
> **You:** Ka ọ dị! *(Goodbye!)*
>
> **Host:** Ka ọ dị, jee nke ọma! *(Goodbye, go well!)*

*New phrase: **Bata** (bah-TAH) = "come in / enter." **Jee nke ọma** = "go well" — a beautiful farewell blessing.*

## Part 3 — Practice Exercise

**Exercise A — Say it out loud (5 minutes).** Stand up, smile, and say each of these three times, exaggerating the tones:
1. Ụtụtụ ọma!
2. Aha m bụ ___ . (your name)
3. Daalụ nke ukwuu!

**Exercise B — Fill the dialogue.** Complete this exchange:
- Stranger: *Ndewo! Kedu aha gị?*
- You: *___* (greet them, say your name, ask theirs back)

**Exercise C — Time-of-day challenge.** It's 8 AM, 2 PM, and 8 PM. Which greeting do you use each time? (Answers: Ụtụtụ ọma / Ehihie ọma / Mgbede ọma.)

**Exercise D — Real-world mission.** Today, greet one person with *Ndewo* or *Kedu?* — a friend, a colleague, anyone. Notice how it feels to say it. That's your first real Igbo conversation. Well done.

## Cultural Note — Respect for Elders

In Igbo culture, how you greet an elder matters as much as the words. A few customs to carry with you:

- **Never rush past an elder's greeting.** If an older person greets you, stop, answer fully, and ask after them. A hurried greeting reads as disrespect.
- **Use titles.** *Mazi* (Mr./sir, for men), *Da* (auntie, for women), *Nna* (father/uncle), *Nne* (mother/auntie). Calling an elder by their first name alone is a faux pas — the title is the respect.
- **Morning greetings carry weight.** Taking the time to say *Ụtụtụ ọma* properly, rather than a quick hello, tells an elder you were raised well.
- **Receive with both hands.** If an elder hands you something (kola nut, a gift, food), accept with both hands or the right hand supported — a small gesture that speaks volumes.
- **Proverb to remember:** *"Onye ajuru aju anaghi aju onwe ya"* — "He who is rejected by others has not rejected himself." In other words: carry yourself with dignity, and greet everyone as if they matter — because they do.
